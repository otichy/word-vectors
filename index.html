Tokenization and normalization preview

Counts use the existing table labels, not an automatically selected vocabulary. Matching is case-insensitive for all languages; accents are preserved. English uses Porter stemming plus common irregular forms (e.g. slept → sleep, ate → eat), applied to both text and table labels. Stems can be fragments and may merge different words. This is not full lemmatization.

Pairs never cross sentence boundaries or line breaks. Apostrophes inside words are retained; English possessive endings are removed before stemming. Hyphenated compounds split into words. Contractions are not expanded. A token is never paired with itself. Sentence boundaries are approximated by periods, question marks, exclamation marks, and line breaks. This also splits abbreviations.

Local detection considers English, Czech, Slovak, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Polish, Russian, and Ukrainian; unsupported or ambiguous input is reported as uncertain. Non-English morphology is never normalized. English settings apply to the whole text; split mixed-language corpora before recounting.

About Porter stemming

Suggestions use your target rows, selected window, and normalization settings. Existing columns and target words are excluded. Function-word filtering affects suggestions only; tokens still occupy window positions.
How suggestions are chosen

Shared contexts occur beside multiple targets and are ranked by target coverage, then frequency. Distinctive contexts have a positive association with a target, ranked by maximum positive pointwise mutual information (PPMI) × log₂(1 + total count). Up to six of each are selected, with remaining slots filled by frequency.

PPMI = max(0, log₂(pair count × all pairs / (target total × context total))). Totals include all observed contexts around the current target rows, before filtering. This measures association within this selected vocabulary, not the whole language. A minimum count reduces rare-pair noise; lower it to 1 for very small examples. Displayed labels are the most frequent surface forms of each normalized context.

Function-word filtering uses small local lists for the detected language. It may miss words; uncertain or unsupported languages have no list. Review suggestions before adding them.

03 From co-occurrence to similarity

Explanation & experiments

Shared evidence, similar pressures

“Cat” and “dog” never occur together in this toy corpus. But both occur with “sleeps” and “eats”. Learning to predict the same contexts gives their target vectors similar pressures. Change “dog” to occur mainly with “moves” and “stops” and compare it with the vehicles.

What the model actually learns

This is a small skip-gram with negative sampling model. It learns two sets of vectors: targets and contexts. An observed pair encourages a higher dot product between its target and context vectors. Three randomly sampled negative pairs encourage lower dot products. Similar target vectors can emerge indirectly from shared contexts.

What the picture means

These are the model’s actual two-dimensional target vectors, not a projection of a larger space. The coordinates have no predetermined meanings such as “animal” or “vehicle”. The origin is marked; vector direction matters for cosine similarity. Emoji are labels only.

  1. Add “horse” and give it the same counts as “cat”.

Model details and limitations

Training uses 120 rounds, two learned dimensions, three negative samples per positive pair, learning rate 0.035, and a deterministic seed. Negative contexts are sampled in proportion to context frequency raised to the power 0.75, with replacement; an observed context may also be sampled as a negative. For tables with up to 10,000 observed pairs, each round visits every pair. Larger tables use 10,000 frequency-weighted positive samples per round to keep training responsive. Corpus recounting uses a fixed symmetric context window; no stopwords are removed.

This toy model uses separate target and context vocabularies, supplied as editable counts or extracted from pasted text. Only words listed in the table are counted as targets or contexts; other tokens still occupy window positions. Real word2vec models typically learn from large corpora in many more dimensions. Results here are illustrative, sensitive to the evidence and training setup, and are not pretrained language knowledge. Opposites may share contexts; a static word vector also mixes different senses of the same spelling. Words with zero counts remain untrained.

Each edit retrains from deterministic initial vectors. All training histories shown are for the current table. The chart uses equal distance per coordinate unit on both axes, with its scale fixed across the current training history. Individual word initialisations depend on their spelling and role; renaming a word changes its initialisation.
